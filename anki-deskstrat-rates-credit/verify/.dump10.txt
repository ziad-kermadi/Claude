===== 0 basic ['cva', 'dva', 'closeout', 'bilateral', 'chain_closeout']
F: <b>[Chain: CLOSEOUT 1/4]</b> Bank B and counterparty C trade an uncollateralised derivative with risk-free pre-default value \(V(t)\). Either party can default; the ISDA close-out is struck at the first default. Write the bilateral value adjustment under independent default times, using first-default densities.
B: <b>Answer:</b> Value adjustment \(=-\text{CVA}+\text{DVA}\), with both loss magnitudes positive. Let \(S_i\) be survival, \(\lambda_i\) the hazard, \(D\) the discount factor, \(V^+,V^-\) the positive and negative parts:<br>
\[\text{CVA}=(1-R_C)\int_0^T D(t)\,E[V^+(t)]\,\lambda_C(t)\,S_C(t)\,S_B(t)\,dt\]
\[\text{DVA}=(1-R_B)\int_0^T D(t)\,E[V^-(t)]\,\lambda_B(t)\,S_B(t)\,S_C(t)\,dt\]
The factor \(\lambda_C S_C S_B\) is the density of "C defaults first"; \(\lambda_B S_B S_C\) is the density of "B defaults first". Only the first default triggers the close-out, so each leg carries the other party's survival.<br>
<b>Interviewer follow-up:</b> Why does the CVA leg carry \(S_B\)? (Next card.)
===== 1 basic ['cva', 'closeout', 'survival', 'chain_closeout']
F: <b>[Chain: CLOSEOUT 2/4]</b> Same bilateral set-up as the previous card. The close-out at the first default is struck at \(V(\tau)\). Why is the CVA weighted by \(S_B(t)\), and what goes wrong if you drop that factor?
B: <b>Answer:</b> The counterparty's default only produces a CVA loss if it happens before the bank's own default. If the bank has already defaulted, the close-out was struck on the bank's default, and the counterparty loss leg is replaced by the DVA leg. The hazard of C is unchanged; what changes is the probability that C's default is the first default, which multiplies the density by \(S_B(t)\).<br>
Dropping \(S_B\) gives the unilateral (naive) CVA, which overstates the counterparty loss and ignores that the bank's own default cancels part of it.<br>
<b>Interviewer follow-up:</b> With constant hazards and zero rates, how big is the \(S_B\) effect? (Next card.)
===== 2 basic ['cva', 'dva', 'closeout', 'case_study', 'chain_closeout']
F: <b>[Chain: CLOSEOUT 3/4]</b> Flat expected positive exposure \(E^+=2\)m and flat \(E^-=1\)m for 5 years, zero rates, \(R_C=R_B=40\%\), CDS spreads 200bp (C) and 100bp (B). Use \(\lambda\approx s/LGD\). Compute CVA, DVA, the bilateral adjustment and the naive unilateral CVA.
B: <b>Steps:</b><br><ul>
<li>\(\lambda_C=0.02/0.6=3.333\%\), \(\lambda_B=0.01/0.6=1.667\%\), \(\Lambda=\lambda_C+\lambda_B=5.0\%\).</li>
<li>With flat exposure and zero rates, \(\int_0^T\lambda_i e^{-\Lambda t}dt=(\lambda_i/\Lambda)(1-e^{-\Lambda T})\), and \(1-e^{-0.25}=0.2212\).</li>
<li>\(\text{CVA}=0.6\times 2\times(3.333/5.0)\times 0.2212=0.1770\)m.</li>
<li>\(\text{DVA}=0.6\times 1\times(1.667/5.0)\times 0.2212=0.0442\)m.</li>
<li>Bilateral adjustment \(=-0.1770+0.0442=-0.1327\)m.</li>
<li>Naive unilateral CVA (no \(S_B\) factor) \(=1.2\times(1-e^{-0.1667})=0.1842\)m.</li></ul>
<b>Sanity check:</b> the \(S_B\) factor cuts CVA by about 4% here. DVA/CVA \(=(E^-/E^+)(\lambda_B/\lambda_C)=0.5\times0.5=0.25\) with equal LGDs, which matches 0.0442/0.1770. Figures are approximate because \(\lambda\approx s/LGD\) is a flat-hazard shortcut.<br>
<b>Interviewer follow-up:</b> Is the bank's DVA a realisable asset? (Next card.)
===== 3 basic ['dva', 'wrong_way_risk', 'hedging', 'chain_closeout']
F: <b>[Chain: CLOSEOUT 4/4]</b> Using the CVA and DVA from the previous card: why does the independence assumption understate risk in a crisis, and why can DVA not be hedged the way CVA is?
B: <b>Answer:</b> Under positive default correlation (common sector or macro shocks), \(S_B\) is low in exactly the states where \(\lambda_C\) is high. The joint density of "C defaults first" then moves with the common state, so the product form \(\lambda_C S_C S_B\) misstates the tail. Independence therefore understates the joint-default region.<br>
For DVA: a bank cannot buy protection on its own default, so it has no direct CDS hedge. A proxy hedge on a sector index hedges a different risk. DVA also produces a P&L gain when the bank's own credit worsens, which is why it is contested, and why some regulatory frameworks exclude it from capital.<br>
<b>Interviewer follow-up:</b> Give one accounting consequence of recognising DVA in P&L. (Own-credit gains appear as income when the bank's spreads widen.)
===== 4 basic ['collateral', 'csa', 'threshold', 'chain_mpor']
F: <b>[Chain: MPOR 1/4]</b> A CSA with threshold \(H=1\)m and minimum transfer amount (MTA) 0.5m. Portfolio MTM \(V=3.2\)m; collateral previously zero. What collateral is called, and what is the net exposure?
B: <b>Answer:</b> Required collateral \(C^{req}=(V-H)^+=2.2\)m. Since 2.2m exceeds the MTA, the call is made for 2.2m. Net exposure \((V-C)^+=\min(V,H)=1.0\)m, i.e. the threshold.<br>
Call rule: call only when \(|C^{req}-C_{prev}|\ge\text{MTA}\). Between calls, net exposure sits at \(H\) plus at most one MTA band. This is the static (no-lag) picture; the next cards add the margin period of risk.<br>
<b>Interviewer follow-up:</b> Does the threshold remove exposure? (It caps it at \(H\); the rest of the risk is lag, next card.)
===== 5 basic ['collateral', 'mpor', 'exposure', 'chain_mpor']
F: <b>[Chain: MPOR 2/4]</b> Same CSA with margin period of risk \(\delta\) (time from last margin call to close-out). For a deal deep in the money at \(t-\delta\), derive the collateralised exposure at default and the Brownian lag exposure with \(H=0\) and no MTA.
B: <b>Answer:</b> Collateral held at \(t\) was called on the MTM at \(t-\delta\): \(C(t)=(V(t-\delta)-H)^+\). Net exposure at default:
\[E_c(t)=\big(V(t)-(V(t-\delta)-H)^+\big)^+\]
If \(V(t-\delta)\ge H\), this reduces to \(E_c=(\Delta V+H)^+\) with \(\Delta V=V(t)-V(t-\delta)\). For \(H=0\) and \(\Delta V\sim N(0,\sigma^2\delta)\):
\[E[E_c]=\frac{\sigma\sqrt{\delta}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\]
So collateral removes everything except the move over one margin period. Paths that start near or below zero add a further term, which this approximation omits.<br>
<b>Interviewer follow-up:</b> Quantify it. (Next card.)

<li>\(s=\sigma\sqrt{\delta}=6\sqrt{10/252}=1.195\)m.</li>
<li>\(EE_{lag}=s/\sqrt{2\pi}=1.195/2.5066=0.477\)m.</li>

===== 7 basic ['collateral', 'mpor', 'regulatory', 'chain_mpor']
F: <b>[Chain: MPOR 4/4]</b> The Brownian lag exposure from the previous card is a simplification. Name what it misses for a realistic portfolio, and state the regulatory margin-period floor for a daily-margined bilateral netting set (approximate; check the current rule text).
B: <b>Answer:</b><br><ul>
<li><b>Jumps and gap risk:</b> an MTM move larger than the threshold plus the lag cannot be collateralised. The \(\sqrt\delta\) scaling understates tail exposure.</li>
<li><b>Disputes and illiquid collateral:</b> they lengthen the effective margin period beyond the nominal \(\delta\).</li>
<li><b>Path maximum within the window:</b> if default occurs inside the window, exposure is the running maximum of the MTM, not its endpoint (Brownian bridge derivation below).</li></ul>

===== 8 basic ['fva', 'funding', 'convention', 'chain_fva']
F: <b>[Chain: FVA 1/4]</b> An uncollateralised derivative: the bank funds positive MTM at spread \(s_F\) and receives a benefit \(s_B\) on negative MTM. Write FCA, FBA and FVA with discounting, and state the sign convention.
B: <b>Answer:</b>
\[\text{FCA}=\int_0^T D(t)\,s_F(t)\,E[V^+(t)]\,dt,\qquad \text{FBA}=\int_0^T D(t)\,s_B(t)\,E[V^-(t)]\,dt\]
FVA as a value adjustment \(=-\text{FCA}+\text{FBA}\). Spreads are measured over the discount (risk-free or OIS) curve. Economic reason: a positive MTM must be funded (a cost per unit time on the balance); a negative MTM is a liability the bank can use as funding (a benefit).<br>
<b>Interviewer follow-up:</b> Why might \(s_F\) and \(s_B\) differ? (Asymmetric funding curves for lending and borrowing, and bank-specific own-default effects.)
===== 9 basic ['fva', 'funding', 'discounting', 'chain_fva']
F: <b>[Chain: FVA 2/4]</b> Same uncollateralised derivative with a symmetric funding spread \(s_F=s_B=s\). What does FVA reduce to, and in what sense is it a discounting effect?
B: <b>Answer:</b>
\[\text{FVA}=-s\int_0^T D(t)\,E[V(t)]\,dt\]
It depends only on the expected net MTM, not on EPE and ENE separately. The funding balance equals the MTM at each date, so the PV of its funding cost is \(-s\) times the PV of the expected balance. That is why it behaves like discounting at \(r+s\): the funding spread accrues on the balance, as a discount rate accrues on a cash flow. The equivalence is a first-order statement about expected balances, not an identity on each path.<br>
<b>Interviewer follow-up:</b> Why does this break down once the spreads are asymmetric? (Positive and negative balances then carry different rates, so the expectation splits into EPE and ENE terms.)
===== 10 basic ['fva', 'case_study', 'funding', 'chain_fva']
F: <b>[Chain: FVA 3/4]</b> Uncollateralised 5-year swap: flat \(E^+=1.5\)m, flat \(E^-=0.5\)m, \(s_F=100\)bp, \(s_B=60\)bp, flat \(r=3\%\). Compute FCA, FBA and net FVA. Compare with a symmetric \(s=100\)bp applied to the net expected MTM \(E^+-E^-=1.0\)m.
B: <b>Steps:</b><br><ul>
<li>Annuity \(A=\int_0^5 e^{-0.03t}dt=(1-e^{-0.15})/0.03=4.6431\).</li>
<li>FCA \(=-0.0100\times1.5\times4.6431=-0.0697\)m.</li>
<li>FBA \(=+0.0060\times0.5\times4.6431=+0.0139\)m.</li>
<li>Net FVA \(=-0.0557\)m, about \(-56\)k.</li>
<li>Symmetric at 100bp on 1.0m net: \(-0.0464\)m.</li></ul>
<b>Sanity check:</b> the asymmetric charge is larger than the symmetric one because it funds the full 1.5m positive exposure, and the benefit only offsets part of it. FBA/FCA \(=(0.6\times0.5)/(1.0\times1.5)=0.2\). Flat exposures are a simplification; path-dependent exposures change the answer.<br>

===== 11 basic ['fva', 'hull_white', 'burgard_kjaer', 'convention', 'chain_fva']
F: <b>[Chain: FVA 4/4]</b> State the Hull–White (2012) objection to FVA and the Burgard–Kjaer (2011) position. Which modelling assumption decides between them?
B: <b>Answer:</b><br>
<b>Hull–White:</b> a trade is valued by discounting its cash flows, with credit adjustments for default. Funding is a firm-level cost. If the funding effect is added on top of the discount and the close-out treatment, it is counted twice, and a charge that depends on the bank's own funding should not change the trade's fair value.<br>
<b>Burgard–Kjaer:</b> the replicating hedge of an uncollateralised derivative is itself funded. The cash flows of that funded hedge are priced, which yields a PDE with a funding term that is not captured by CVA or DVA alone.<br>
<b>Decisive assumption:</b> whether a market participant prices the funding of the replicating hedge into the trade (the exit-price view, supporting Burgard–Kjaer), or whether funding is a firm-level cost that should not enter the trade value (Hull–White). Practice has largely adopted the first view with DVA/FBA overlap controls. This is a simplified summary.<br>
<b>Interviewer follow-up:</b> Where does the bank's own default enter in the Burgard–Kjaer PDE? (Through the close-out and the DVA term; see the PDE derivation card.)
===== 12 basic ['mva', 'initial_margin', 'funding', 'chain_mva']
F: <b>[Chain: MVA 1/3]</b> A bilateral uncleared trade requires initial margin \(IM(t)\) to be posted and funded for the life of the trade. Write the MVA as a discounted funding cost on the expected IM.
B: <b>Answer:</b>
\[\text{MVA}=-\int_0^T D(t)\,s_F(t)\,E[IM(t)]\,dt\]
IM is a funded balance that is posted at inception and returned at close-out or maturity. Its funding cost accrues on \(E[IM]\) at the bank's posting spread \(s_F\), regardless of the MTM sign. Posted IM is segregated and cannot be reused, so the bank pays a funding cost on it. The sign and spread convention match the FCA term.<br>
<b>Interviewer follow-up:</b> Does MVA depend on the MTM? (No; IM depends on the risk profile, next card.)
===== 13 basic ['mva', 'initial_margin', 'simm', 'chain_mva']
F: <b>[Chain: MVA 2/3]</b> Why is \(E[IM(t)]\) not a function of today's MTM, and why does an at-the-money swap still carry material MVA?
B: <b>Answer:</b> Standard IM (ISDA SIMM) is a sensitivity-based risk measure: it aggregates delta, vega and curvature across risk classes and buckets with supervisory correlations. It therefore depends on the portfolio's risk profile (DV01, vol exposure, tenor distribution), not on its MTM.<br>
An at-the-money 10-year swap has near-zero MTM but a large DV01 and so a large IM. Its IM decays as the remaining duration shrinks. The MVA profile follows the risk-run-off profile, not the EE profile. For long-dated uncleared trades MVA can be comparable to or larger than CVA (approx.).<br>
<b>Interviewer follow-up:</b> How do you compute \(E[IM(t)]\) along Monte Carlo paths? (Next card.)
===== 14 basic ['mva', 'regression', 'simm', 'monte_carlo', 'chain_mva']
F: <b>[Chain: MVA 3/3]</b> You need \(E[IM(t)]\) on Monte Carlo paths, but SIMM is a full sensitivity-based calculation. How do you project it, and what error does the projection introduce into MVA?
B: <b>Answer:</b> Nested simulation (a SIMM calculation for every path and every date) is too expensive. The standard approach:<br>
<ol><li>Simulate risk factors and the sensitivity vector (bucketed deltas, vegas, curvature) at each date.</li>
<li>On a training set of paths, regress the computed SIMM on a low-dimensional feature set (bucket sensitivities, DV01, vol proxies), with basis functions suited to the concave, square-root-type aggregation.</li>
<li>Evaluate the fitted function on all paths and integrate.</li></ol>
Because MVA is linear in \(E[IM]\), what matters is that the regression is unbiased for the conditional mean \(E[IM\mid\text{features}]\). Errors from concentration and correlation effects appear as bias, and feature-set choice trades speed against bias and overfitting. Validate on out-of-sample paths and at the tails.<br>
<b>Interviewer follow-up:</b> Why is the residual's zero mean not enough on its own? (The regression must be unbiased conditionally on the features, not just on average over all paths.)
===== 15 basic ['kva', 'capital', 'hurdle', 'convention', 'chain_kva']
F: <b>[Chain: KVA 1/3]</b> A trade consumes regulatory capital \(K(t)\) over its life. Write KVA with discounting at the risk-free rate \(r\) and a shareholder hurdle \(k\). What is the charge rate \(h\)?
B: <b>Answer:</b>
\[\text{KVA}=\int_0^T D(t)\,h\,K(t)\,dt,\qquad h=k-r\]
Discounting at \(r\) already credits the risk-free return on the capital. Shareholders require \(k\), so the excess \(k-r\) is the economic cost. Charging the full \(k\) on top of discounting at \(r\) double counts the risk-free carry.<br>
<b>Interviewer follow-up:</b> What if your discount curve is the funding curve? (Then \(h=k-\text{funding rate}\), and the KVA/FVA overlap appears; next card.)
===== 16 basic ['kva', 'fva', 'double_counting', 'capital', 'chain_kva']
F: <b>[Chain: KVA 2/3]</b> Where does KVA overlap with FVA, and how do desks avoid counting the same economic cost twice?
B: <b>Answer:</b> Regulatory capital is equity-funded. If the funding curve used in FVA already embeds an equity-like cost, or if FVA charges funding on a balance that includes the capital buffer, the same cost appears in both adjustments.<br>
Two common conventions:<br>
<ol><li>FVA on the funded MTM and IM only; KVA on capital at \(h=k-r\) with \(r\) the discount rate. The capital is not in the funding balance.</li>
<li>If the funding curve already includes the equity cost, drop the excess-hurdle term, or set \(h\) relative to the funding rate.</li></ol>
Trade-off: convention consistency across the book versus alignment with the bank's capital plan. The right \(h\) is the marginal equity cost, which rises in stress, so the hurdle is itself a model-risk input.<br>
<b>Interviewer follow-up:</b> Which hurdle is appropriate for a bank under capital stress? (The marginal, not average, cost of equity; a model-risk assumption.)

===== 18 basic ['cva', 'delta', 'hedging', 'rates', 'chain_cva_hedge']
F: <b>[Chain: CVA_HEDGE 1/3]</b> An uncollateralised receiver swap has MTM \(V(r)\) with \(\partial V/\partial r<0\). The CVA loss \(L(r)=\text{LGD}\,E[D\,V^+]\) rises when rates fall. What is the sign of the CVA rate delta relative to the swap's own delta, and what does this mean for the desk?
B: <b>Answer:</b> \(\partial L/\partial r<0\) for the receiver: the exposure, and therefore the loss, grows as rates fall. The adjusted value is \(V-L\), so \(\partial(V-L)/\partial r=V'-L'\). Since \(L'<0\), the term \(-L'\) is positive, so CVA partially offsets the swap's own delta: the adjusted DV01 is smaller in magnitude than the trade DV01.<br>
For a payer, \(V'>0\) and \(L'>0\), and the same offset applies. General rule: for a one-directional position, CVA delta reduces the position's own delta.<br>
Desk consequence: hedging the trade DV01 without the CVA delta overhedges. The adjusted book must be hedged on a net basis.<br>
<b>Interviewer follow-up:</b> When can the offset fail? (When the exposure sits on the other side of the book, for example after a large offsetting trade, so the relevant \(V^+\) region has the opposite rate sensitivity.)
===== 19 basic ['cva', 'gamma', 'cross_gamma', 'rates', 'chain_cva_hedge']
F: <b>[Chain: CVA_HEDGE 2/3]</b> Take \(L=\text{LGD}\int_0^T E[V^+(t;r)]\,\lambda e^{-\lambda t}D(t)\,dt\) with constant counterparty hazard \(\lambda\). Derive the rates–credit cross-gamma (ignore the rate dependence of \(D\)), and give its sign for a receiver.
B: <b>Answer:</b> Differentiating in \(\lambda\) first: \(\partial L/\partial\lambda=\text{LGD}\int E[V^+]\,(1-\lambda t)e^{-\lambda t}D\,dt\), since \(\frac{d}{d\lambda}(\lambda e^{-\lambda t})=(1-\lambda t)e^{-\lambda t}\). Then in \(r\):
\[\frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial r\,\partial\lambda}=\text{LGD}\int_0^T\frac{\partial E[V^+]}{\partial r}\,(1-\lambda t)\,e^{-\lambda t}D\,dt\]
For a receiver \(\partial E[V^+]/\partial r<0\). Over the bulk of the integrand, \(\lambda t<1\), so \((1-\lambda t)>0\), and the cross-gamma is negative. Interpretation: when rates fall and spreads widen together, the CVA loss rises by more than the sum of the individual moves.<br>
Desk consequence: a joint rates-down, spreads-wide scenario produces P&L that delta hedges on each factor alone do not capture. Run joint rates and credit grids.<br>
<b>Interviewer follow-up:</b> When does the sign flip? (When \(\lambda t>1\) dominates the weight, which needs long horizons or high hazards.)
===== 20 basic ['cva', 'hedging', 'cds', 'proxy', 'basis', 'chain_cva_hedge']
F: <b>[Chain: CVA_HEDGE 3/3]</b> The counterparty has no liquid CDS. You hedge its spread CS01 with a sector index whose daily spread changes have \(\sigma_i=6\)bp, while the counterparty's have \(\sigma_c=8\)bp and correlation \(\rho=0.7\). What index hedge ratio minimises variance, and what fraction of variance remains?
B: <b>Answer:</b> Minimise \(\text{Var}(\Delta s_c-h\,\Delta s_i)=\sigma_c^2-2h\rho\sigma_c\sigma_i+h^2\sigma_i^2\). The first-order condition gives
\[h^*=\frac{\rho\,\sigma_c}{\sigma_i}=\frac{0.7\times8}{6}=0.93\]
Residual variance fraction \(1-\rho^2=0.51\), so the hedge removes 49% of variance. The rest is basis (idiosyncratic) risk.<br>
<b>Where it breaks:</b> the correlation is unstable and typically rises in stress; a jump-to-default of the counterparty is not covered by the index hedge; and \(h^*\) estimated from history is noisy.<br>
<b>Interviewer follow-up:</b> Why is the 49% hedge effectiveness not improved by a larger index notional? (Minimum variance is at \(h^*\); larger notional adds risk.)
===== 21 basic ['cva', 'derivation', 'expected_loss', 'closeout']
F: Derive unilateral CVA as an expected loss, starting from the default indicator and ending at the discrete sum. State the assumption used at each step.
B: <b>Steps:</b><br><ol>
<li><b>Loss at default.</b> For counterparty default at \(\tau\le T\), the loss is \((1-R_C)D(\tau)V^+(\tau)\mathbf 1_{\tau\le T}\). Assumes close-out at the value \(V(\tau)\) and recovery \(R_C\) on the positive part.</li>
<li><b>Compensator.</b> Let \(J_t=\mathbf 1_{\tau\le t}\) with intensity \(\lambda\). Then \(J_t-\int_0^t\lambda(s)(1-J_{s-})ds\) is a \(Q\)-martingale. Assumes a doubly stochastic default time.</li>
<li><b>Expectation.</b> \(\text{Loss}=\int_0^T(1-R_C)D(t)V^+(t)\,dJ_t\), so \(\text{CVA}=(1-R_C)\int_0^T E[D(t)V^+(t)(1-J_{t-})]\lambda(t)\,dt\).</li>
<li><b>Independence.</b> Let \(V\) be independent of \(\tau\) (no wrong-way risk). Then \(E[(1-J_{t-})\,\cdot\,]\) factorises into \(S(t)\) and \(\text{CVA}=(1-R_C)\int_0^T E[D V^+]\lambda S\,dt\).</li>
<li><b>Discretise.</b> On \(0=t_0<\dots<t_N\): \(\text{CVA}\approx(1-R_C)\sum_i E[D(t_i)V^+(t_i)]\,[S(t_{i-1})-S(t_i)]\), evaluating exposure at the bucket end. This is a first-order grid approximation.</li></ol>
<b>Where it breaks:</b> the independence step fails under wrong-way risk; the \(V\) used is the collateralised close-out value, not the MTM; and the bucket-end rule biases the answer when exposure varies quickly within a bucket.
===== 22 basic ['fva', 'dva', 'cva', 'pde', 'derivation', 'burgard_kjaer']
F: Sketch the Burgard–Kjaer PDE for a derivative with bilateral default and asymmetric funding. Start from a value \(V(t,x)\) with jump-to-default for each party, and identify the CVA, DVA and FVA terms.
B: <b>Steps:</b><br><ol>
<li><b>Feynman–Kac with jumps.</b> Under \(Q\), with generator \(\mathcal L\) of the state \(x\), the value satisfies
\[(\partial_t+\mathcal L)V-rV+\lambda_C\,(V_C-V)+\lambda_B\,(V_B-V)+f=0\]
where \(V_C,V_B\) are the post-default values and \(f\) is the funding term.</li>
<li><b>Close-out post-default values.</b> \(V_C=V-(1-R_C)V^+\) (the bank recovers \(R_C\) on its claim); \(V_B=V+(1-R_B)V^-\) (the bank pays only \(R_B\) on its liability).</li>
<li><b>Substitute.</b> \(\lambda_C(V_C-V)=-\lambda_C(1-R_C)V^+\) and \(\lambda_B(V_B-V)=+\lambda_B(1-R_B)V^-\), giving
\[(\partial_t+\mathcal L)V-rV-\lambda_C(1-R_C)V^++\lambda_B(1-R_B)V^-+f=0\]</li>
<li><b>Funding term.</b> In its simplest form \(f=-s_FV^++s_BV^-\), a cost on positive values and a benefit on negative values (sign convention as in the FCA/FBA chain).</li>
<li><b>Read off.</b> The \(\lambda_C\) term is the CVA, the \(\lambda_B\) term the DVA, and \(f\) the FVA. Nonlinearity through \(V^\pm\) means the PDE is solved numerically, for example by finite differences with policy iteration on the sign of \(V\).</li></ol>
<b>Where it breaks:</b> the close-out is taken on the risk-free value \(V\), so the funding cost of the hedge does not feed back into the close-out amount; a fuller treatment, where it does, is the source of the Hull–White objection. The constant-\(s\) funding term is also a simplification.
===== 23 basic ['mpor', 'brownian_bridge', 'derivation', 'exposure']
F: Let \(X\) be a Brownian motion with volatility \(\sigma\), \(X_0=x\), \(X_\Delta=y\). Derive \(P(\max_{[0,\Delta]}X\ge b\mid X_0=x,X_\Delta=y)\) for \(b\ge\max(x,y)\), and state how it is used for an MPoR window between two simulation nodes.
B: <b>Steps:</b><br><ol>
<li><b>Standardise.</b> Set \(x=0\), \(\sigma=1\); restore later.</li>
<li><b>Reflection principle.</b> For \(b\ge\max(0,y)\), \(P(M_\Delta\ge b,\,X_\Delta\in dy)=\varphi_\Delta(2b-y)\,dy\), where \(\varphi_\Delta\) is the \(N(0,\Delta)\) density. The path is reflected at the first hitting time of \(b\).</li>
<li><b>Condition.</b> Divide by \(\varphi_\Delta(y)\):
\[P(M\ge b\mid X_\Delta=y)=\frac{\varphi_\Delta(2b-y)}{\varphi_\Delta(y)}=\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(2b-y)^2-y^2}{2\Delta}\Big)=\exp\!\Big(-\frac{2b(b-y)}{\Delta}\Big)\]</li>
<li><b>Restore.</b> \(P=\exp\!\big(-2(b-x)(b-y)/(\sigma^2\Delta)\big)\).</li>
<li><b>Use.</b> Between two nodes with known MTMs, the chance of breaching a collateral or close-out level inside the window follows from this formula; integrating over the level gives the expected peak exposure within the window.</li></ol>
Numerical check: \(x=y=0\), \(b=\sigma\sqrt\Delta\) gives \(P=e^{-2}=0.135\). A path that starts and ends at zero touches +1 standard deviation inside the window 13.5% of the time, so a coarse grid misses peaks.<br>
<b>Where it breaks:</b> the formula assumes constant \(\sigma\), no drift and continuous monitoring. Discrete monitoring lowers the true hitting probability (continuity correction needed), and jumps in MTM are not captured.
===== 24 basic ['wrong_way_risk', 'derivation', 'copula', 'cva']
F: Set exposure \(V=-\sigma Z\) with \(Z\) standard normal, and let default occur when \(X=\rho Z+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\varepsilon<k\) with \(\Phi(k)=p\). Derive \(E[V\mid\text{default}]\), then evaluate for \(p=2\%\), \(\rho=0.5\), \(\sigma=5\)m.
B: <b>Steps:</b><br><ol>
<li><b>Stein's lemma.</b> \((Z,X)\) are jointly standard normal with \(\text{Cov}(Z,X)=\rho\). Then \(E[Z\,g(X)]=\rho\,E[g'(X)]\). With \(g=\mathbf 1_{x<k}\), \(g'=-\delta_k\), so \(E[Z\,\mathbf 1_{X<k}]=-\rho\,\varphi(k)\).</li>
<li><b>Conditional mean.</b> Divide by \(P(X<k)=p\): \(E[Z\mid D]=-\rho\,\varphi(k)/\Phi(k)\).</li>
<li><b>Exposure.</b> \(E[V\mid D]=-\sigma E[Z\mid D]=\sigma\rho\,\varphi(k)/\Phi(k)\), an inverse Mills ratio.</li>
<li><b>Numbers.</b> \(k=\Phi^{-1}(0.02)=-2.054\), \(\varphi(k)=0.0484\), \(\varphi(k)/p=2.421\). Then \(E[V\mid D]=5\times0.5\times2.421=6.05\)m. Unconditionally \(E[V]=0\).</li></ol>
<b>Sanity check:</b> the default-state exposure is positive and of the order of one standard deviation of the MTM, which is the wrong-way effect. The independence formula would carry an exposure of zero here.<br>
<b>Where it breaks:</b> the result is for a linear Gaussian MTM; CVA needs \(E[V^+\mid D]\), not \(E[V\mid D]\), and a Gaussian copula understates joint tail dependence compared with a Student-t copula.
===== 25 basic ['incremental_xva', 'euler', 'derivation', 'netting']
F: Two trades have future MTMs \(V_A,V_B\) with zero mean, \(\sigma_A=\sigma_B=2\)m, \(\rho=-0.5\). Derive the Euler (marginal) allocation of portfolio EE to each trade, verify the sum rule, and compare with standalone EE.
B: <b>Steps:</b><br><ol>
<li><b>Portfolio EE.</b> With weights \(w_A,w_B\), \(\mu=0\): \(EE(w)=E[(w_AV_A+w_BV_B)^+]=\varphi(0)\,\sigma_p(w)\), where \(\sigma_p^2=w_A^2\sigma_A^2+w_B^2\sigma_B^2+2\rho w_Aw_B\sigma_A\sigma_B\).</li>
<li><b>Euler derivative.</b> \(\partial EE/\partial w_A=\varphi(0)\,(w_A\sigma_A^2+\rho w_B\sigma_A\sigma_B)/\sigma_p\). Similarly for \(B\).</li>
<li><b>Sum rule.</b> EE is homogeneous of degree one in \((w_A,w_B)\), so by Euler's theorem \(EE=w_A\,\partial_AEE+w_B\,\partial_BEE\). The marginal contributions add to the total.</li>
<li><b>Numbers.</b> \(\sigma_p=\sqrt{4+4-4}=2\)m. Portfolio EE \(=0.399\times2=0.798\)m. Euler: \(\varphi(0)(4-2)/2=0.399\)m for each trade, sum 0.798m. Standalone: 0.798m each, sum 1.596m.</li></ol>
<b>Sanity check:</b> the standalone sum is double the portfolio EE, which is the netting and diversification benefit. The marginal contribution of each trade is half its standalone EE.<br>
<b>Where it breaks:</b> with non-zero \(\mu\), use \(\partial EE=\Phi(\mu/\sigma)\partial\mu+\varphi(\mu/\sigma)\partial\sigma\). Monte Carlo marginals are noisy, so use pathwise or smoothed estimators. PFE-based allocation requires a homogeneous quantile measure.
===== 26 basic ['aad', 'sensitivities', 'monte_carlo', 'derivation']
F: Explain why adjoint algorithmic differentiation (AAD) makes full XVA sensitivities affordable. Give the cost scaling and the three main places AAD breaks down for XVA.
B: <b>Answer:</b><br><ol>
<li><b>Cost.</b> Reverse mode computes the gradient of one scalar output (the XVA) with respect to \(N\) inputs at a cost of a small constant multiple of one forward valuation, independent of \(N\). Bump-and-revalue costs \(N+1\) full valuations. The multiple is commonly a few times the forward cost (approx.).</li>

<b>Where it breaks:</b><br><ul>
<li>Non-differentiable operations: digital and barrier payoffs, the \(\max\) and \(\min\) of close-out and collateral rules, and indicator functions. These need smoothing, pathwise, or likelihood-ratio treatment.</li>
<li>Least-squares Monte Carlo exposure models: the regression coefficients must be differentiated through, or frozen with a justified approximation.</li>

===== 27 basic ['exposure', 'epe', 'eepe', 'basel', 'convention']

===== 28 basic ['pfe', 'exposure', 'risk_measure', 'limits']

===== 36 basic ['dva', 'fba', 'double_counting', 'bilateral']
